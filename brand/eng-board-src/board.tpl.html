The language and writing strands, and the mascot

No course-wide frame, as you asked. Each strand has a look of its own, and every grade uses it. Language covers comprehension, summary, visual literacy, language in context and editing. Writing covers essays, transactional pieces and process writing. Literature gets per-work art on the sibling board. Every writing look is the sibling of the language look with the same number: the same paper, ink and type family, used for a different job. So pick a number, not two separate looks. The texts in the art are set in the pupils' own media: posts, short-video ads, gamer blogs. Every app, brand and account is made up.

Language strand: three options

Each shows a lesson opener, a worked example (a text with marks and callouts), an aside, and the accent for each grade.

L1 Tracing overlay

The text sits on studio paper. A sheet of tracing paper is taped over it, and the analysis is drawn on that sheet: cobalt construction lines, outlined words, numbered leader lines, and one signal-red line for the key idea. It is precise and calm, and it looks like a designer taking a text apart.

Worked example · 2 marks

Comment on the tone of this post.

Oh GREAT.1 Monday. Literally2 my fave. Can't wait. Truly.3

  1. 1Capital letters and a full stop make the praise loud but flat. It says the opposite of what it means.
  2. 2The casual, exaggerated word choice makes the post sound mocking, not sincere.
  3. 3A one-word sentence lands the joke with a deadpan finish.

Answer: The tone is sarcastic ✓: "GREAT" and "Truly" praise Monday while the writer clearly dreads it ✓.

Examiner's eyeName the tone in one word, then prove it with a quotation. A tone word on its own scores nothing.

L2 Specimen cabinet

Words and ideas are collected like natural-history specimens. They are pinned on cream card, labelled in typewriter type and sorted into trays and jars. Summary points become specimens, and an example is something you leave out of the tray. It is warm and bookish, and the method makes sense to pupils.

Worked example · point-finding

Find the points. Leave the examples behind.

Your phone is not just a clock. Each ping nudges your brain awake2, even when you don't check it. The glow tells your body it is still daytime3, so sleep comes later. My cousin once scrolled until 4 a.m. and fell asleep in a maths test.

  1. 2Notifications wake the brain. (In your own words: 4 words.)
  2. 3Screen light delays sleep. (4 words)
  3. ×The cousin is an example, not a point. It stays out of the tray.

Running count: 8 / 90 words · points found: 2 / 7

Curator's ruleAn example is never a point. If you can take it out and the argument still stands, take it out.

L3 Contact sheet

A photographer's darkroom. Texts and adverts appear as frames on a strip of film, and a loupe magnifies the detail that matters. Safelight red and amber notes do the analysis. It is made for visual literacy (adverts, campaigns, cartoons), and it gives comprehension a cinematic, moody feel.

Worked example · 3 marks

How does the advert target its audience? Refer to the copy.

NEVER LOG OFF.1 ZAP: energy for the next round.2 *Not for under-16s3

  1. 1The imperative and the absolute "never" turn staying awake into a challenge.
  2. 2Gaming slang ("the next round") speaks straight to young gamers.
  3. 3The small print says the opposite of what the pictures show: a hint that the advert knows its real audience.

Answer: gamer slang and a dare-like imperative aim at teens, while the tiny warning lets the brand deny it.

Safelight tipRead the small print last, but quote it first. That is where adverts hide what they are really doing.

Writing strand: three options

Each shows a lesson opener, a draft with edits, an aside, and the grade accents (the same as its sibling language look).

W1 Drafting table sibling of L1

The same studio paper and cobalt grid, but now the pupil builds instead of taking apart. Drafts are stacked sheets with version tabs, the essay's structure is drawn as a layout with construction lines, and the red proof-reader's marks (caret, delete, new paragraph) show the edits.

Draft v2 → v3 · body paragraph

In my opinion Phones can distract alot a great deal1, but a ban is stupid and2 ignores how often we use them to look things up, check a timetable or translate a word3.

  1. 1"A lot" is two words, and "a great deal" suits the formal register better.
  2. 2"Stupid" is emotive. A discursive essay weighs both sides; it does not sneer at one.
  3. 3Three concrete uses turn the claim into evidence.

W2 Field notebook sibling of L2

The specimen collector's own notebook: ruled cream pages, an elastic band, a pressed fynbos sprig, and drafts kept as dated entries. Formats are drawn like field sketches with handwritten oxblood notes, and edits are crossings-out and insertions in the margin. Warm and personal, it fits process writing.

Entry 3 · second draft

Dear whoever bystanders,
You guys film it, you post it, and then like and then you scroll on. Tonight someone's worst day is your content.

"guys" and "like" are chat, not letter. Keep the anger, lose the slang. The last line is your strongest, so end a paragraph on it.

W3 Developing tray sibling of L3

The darkroom again, but now the prints are the pupil's drafts. Draft 1 hangs faint on the line, draft 2 is coming through, and the final copy is sharp. Process writing becomes something you can see: each draft develops for longer. Edits show in amber under the safelight.

Draft 2 → final · opening line

This blog will discuss my experience of not playing ranked games for one month. Honestly? I didn't miss it. For thirty days I logged in, looked at the queue, and logged out again, and nobody noticed but me.

  1. 1A blog starts with a voice, not an announcement. Cut the "this blog will discuss" line.
  2. 2The rhetorical question sets a chatty register that suits the readers.
  3. 3The added clause gives the post a personal edge.

Mascots: three candidates

Each candidate is drawn in layers: base, shadow, highlight and a cool rim light, with the light coming from the top left. All three are shown greeting, reacting to a mistake, and celebrating. They are South African animals, and none of them is the hadeda.

{{RATEL}}

Ratel honey badger · the editor

Fearless and blunt, and secretly kind. Ratel wears a green editor's visor and never puts down the red pencil. It will chew through any sentence, but it never bites the writer.

Nags about
comma splices, waffle and "very", going over the word limit, unclear "it" and "they", the apostrophe in "it's"
Grades
One Ratel for all three grades. Only the visor changes, in the grade accent (cobalt, teal, violet, or the family's colours). Ratel's look stays the same, so pupils recognise it.
Fits
Language strand: editing, summary and language in context
{{FOLIO}}

Folio ground pangolin · the archivist

Shy and precise. Folio keeps every quotation filed and curls up at sloppy evidence. Pangolins really do walk on their hind legs, so Folio always holds something: a book, a card or a scroll.

Nags about
summaries over 90 words, examples posing as points, quotations without the line, retelling the plot instead of answering
Grades
Suits a family. In Gr 10 a pup rides on Folio's tail, as pangolin young really do. In Gr 11 the pup walks alongside, and in Gr 12 it has grown and has its own glasses. It is a gentle way to show the pupils growing up.
Fits
Language strand and the literature essays (evidence and quotation)
{{QUILL}}

Quill secretary bird · the stickler

Tall, formal and theatrical, with a red bow tie. The crest is a fan of quill pens, and one ends in a gold nib. Real secretary birds stamp on snakes; Quill stamps on clichés.

Nags about
clichés ("at the end of the day"), a missing salutation or sign-off, register slips, one-sentence paragraphs, the wrong format for the task
Grades
One bird, but the crest grows a quill each year: four in Gr 10, five in Gr 11, and six with the gold nib in Gr 12. It is a quiet "level up" pupils can spot.
Fits
Writing strand: essays, transactional writing and orals

A thought before you choose: one mascot per strand also works. Ratel could nag in the language sections and Quill in the writing sections, which would make the strand switch even clearer. Folio would suit the literature essays if you want a third. Or pick one of them to cover the whole course.

How the strands fit

The course page and lesson list show each lesson in its strand's look. A small swatch and icon sit on every row: the chosen family's paper for language and its sibling for writing. Literature rows use the work's own colour from the literature board. The list scrolls like a varied timetable, not like one wallpaper.

Language lessons (comprehension, summary, visual literacy, language in context, editing) all use the L look. The grade accent changes the lines, pins or safelight, so a Gr 11 page still feels like Gr 11.

Writing lessons (essays, transactional, process writing and drafts) use the W sibling. It has the same type family and paper, so a pupil can tell it is the same course but a different kind of work.

Literature units use the per-work looks from the sibling board: Elsinore for Hamlet, the Pacific for Life of Pi, and so on. A literature essay lesson keeps the work's art for its pictures, but its draft-and-edit cards use the W look, so the writing skills look the same wherever they appear.

The mascot crosses every strand unchanged. It is the one constant, and it carries the margin jokes as doodles.
