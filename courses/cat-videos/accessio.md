## cat11-03.1 Devices for users with disabilities (about 7 min)

**Goes:** after section `#everyone` - the comment after `w3Hands`.
**The pupil can afterwards:** explain the idea of replacing a missing sense or movement, name hardware for blind, deaf and physically disabled users and say how each is used, and plan devices for one person step by step.
**Thumbnail:** tag `CAT · HARDWARE`, title "A computer for *everyone*"

### Scenes

1. **Hook (0:00-0:40).** Hi, and welcome to BestLessons. Ayanda joins Ms Naidoo's CAT class next term. He is blind. How will he type an essay - and read back what he typed?
   > Board: an empty desk in the CAT room with Ayanda's name card; Clicky scratching its head.
2. **The idea (0:40-1:40).** A keyboard needs hands and eyes, a mouse a steady hand, a screen eyes, a speaker ears. Make the computer accessible: use a sense or movement the person has in place of the one they do not - touch or hearing for sight, sight for hearing, eyes, head, voice, feet or breath for hands. Settings and programs are lesson 7; this is the hardware.
   > Board: three arrows - eye to hand and ear, ear to eye, hand to eye, head, voice, foot and breath. Yellow highlighter on "accessible".
3. **Blind or poor sight (1:40-3:00).** A braille display: pins pop up to form braille dots, read with the fingertips. A braille embosser: a printer that punches braille into thick paper. Headphones for a screen reader. A large-print keyboard and a large monitor for people who can see a little. Louis Braille made his alphabet at 15, in 1824.
   > Board: the braille-keyboard picture; pins rising under a fingertip.
4. **Deaf or hard of hearing (3:00-3:40).** Show the sound on the screen: captions and a flash instead of a beep; vibration on a phone; Bluetooth hearing aids.
   > Board: a phone vibrating; a caption bar under a video.
5. **Hands and arms (3:40-5:40).** Trackball - stays still, the thumb rolls the ball. Keyguard - a hole above each key for shaking hands. Large-key keyboard. Foot mouse. Eye tracker - a camera follows the eyes, looking at a button clicks it. Head mouse - a sticker on the forehead. Sip-and-puff - a tube to sip or blow into. A switch - one big button while choices light up one by one. A microphone for voice recognition. Stephen Hawking chose words with one cheek muscle.
   > Board: Clicky demonstrating each in turn, ending by blowing into a tube.
6. **Choosing for one person (5:40-6:40).** Ms Naidoo's five steps for Ayanda: what can he not do; what can he do instead; input - his keyboard or braille keys; output - headphones for a screen reader and a braille display to check spelling; settings and software. A braille display costs from about R20 000 - start with what the computer already has.
   > Board: the five steps on sticky notes, ticked off one by one.
7. **Sign-off.**

## cat11-03.2 dpi, ppm and megapixels - reading the box (about 9 min)

**Goes:** after section `#cameras` - the comment after `r3Print`.
**The pupil can afterwards:** say what decides the quality of a screen, a printer and a camera; read dpi, ppm (and, for CAPS, colour depth and duty cycle) on an advert; fix a blurry screen; and work out a print time and a print size.
**Thumbnail:** tag `CAT · HARDWARE`, title "What do the numbers on the *box* mean?"

### Scenes

1. **Hook (0:00-0:30).** Hi, and welcome to BestLessons. Two printer adverts on Ms Naidoo's desk: "1200 dpi", "40 ppm", "duty cycle 80 000 pages". Which is better? Let's read the box.
   > Board: two printer boxes covered in numbers; Clicky with a magnifying glass.
2. **Screens (0:30-2:30).** From Grade 10: resolution, aspect ratio, refresh rate. Sharpness is resolution and size together - Full HD on 14 inches is crisp, on 32 inches soft up close. The native resolution, marked Recommended: anything else is stretched and blurry. Text too small? Keep the resolution and change Scale.
   > Board: the cat-screen-bigger-not-sharper drawing.
   > Screen: in the VM, right-click the desktop, Display settings, Display resolution (highlight "(Recommended)"), then Scale to 150%.
3. **dpi (2:30-3:50).** Dots per inch - dots in a line one inch (2.54 cm) long. More dots, sharper print. 600 dpi is plenty for text; lasers print 600 or 1200; photo inkjets say 4800 x 1200. Scanners: 300 dpi for documents, 600 or more for photos to enlarge.
   > Board: a letter "a" printed at low and high dpi, side by side. Yellow highlighter on "dots per inch".
4. **ppm (3:50-5:00).** Pages per minute. Home inkjet 10 to 20; office laser 30 to 60; colour slower than black. "Up to" is the best case. 120 pages at 30 ppm: 120 divided by 30 is 4 minutes, at best.
   > Board: the choose-up-to doodle; the sum worked on a sticky note.
5. **Colour depth and duty cycle - CAPS (5:00-6:30).** Colour depth: how many colours each pixel can show - 16.7 million (8-bit), about a billion (10-bit); too few and a sky shows stripes. Duty cycle: the most pages a month a printer is built for; the recommended monthly volume is much lower. Choose a printer whose recommended volume beats your busy month.
   > Board: a striped sky beside a smooth one; a printer gasping under a mountain of paper. Note on screen: "CAPS".
6. **Cameras (6:30-8:30).** From Grade 10: megapixels, optical vs digital zoom. Also: a bigger sensor catches more light for clearer photos in the dark; a good lens; video in 1080p or 4K at 30 or 60 fps, 4K about four times the storage. Printing: 300 pixels per inch - 4000 x 3000 pixels is about 13 x 10 inches, 34 x 25 cm, bigger than A4. A Full HD screen shows only about 2 megapixels.
   > Board: the zoom-with-feet doodle; the print-size sum on a sticky note.
7. **Sign-off.**
