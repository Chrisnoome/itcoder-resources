## cat10-16.1 What you really buy when you buy software (about 8 min)

**Goes:** after the sort question `g16Models`, at the end of section
`#freeShare`.
**The pupil can afterwards:** say what a licence and a EULA are, explain
source code, tell proprietary, open-source, freeware and shareware apart
with an example of each, and give an advantage and a disadvantage of
proprietary and of open-source software.
**Thumbnail:** tag `CAT · SOFTWARE`, title "You never *own* the game"

### Scenes

1. **Hook (0:00-0:40).** Hi, and welcome to BestLessons. Thabo pays R600 for a
   game. A box with 47 pages and an I agree button pops up. He clicks it in
   under a second. What did he agree to - and what did he actually buy?
   > Board: a very long scroll unrolling off the screen; Clicky clicking
   > I AGREE without looking.
2. **A licence (0:40-1:40).** A copy of software costs almost nothing, so the
   company keeps owning it and sells permission with rules. Thabo may play
   the game on his account; he may not copy it for friends or sell copies.
   > Board: a game box with a tag "PERMISSION, WITH RULES"; yellow band on
   > "permission".
3. **The EULA and installing (1:40-2:50).** End-user licence agreement: the
   end user is you. What it usually says: how many devices, no copying,
   sharing, selling or changing, not responsible for damage, data
   collected, student versions not for profit. The six install steps: run
   the installer, the EULA appears, read it, I agree (Cancel stops it), the
   product key, it activates.
   > Board: the six steps as a numbered strip; pink highlighter on "Cancel
   > = no software".
4. **Source code - the recipe (2:50-3:40).** The program as the programmer
   wrote it, readable by people. The cake and the recipe: with the cake you
   can eat it; with the recipe you can see what's in it, bake your own and
   change it.
   > Board: a cake (the program) and a recipe card (source code).
5. **Proprietary (3:40-4:50).** Owned by a company, recipe secret, you pay for
   a licence: Windows, Microsoft Office, Photoshop, most games. For: support,
   polished, widely used, training easy to find. Against: costs money, can't
   change it, depends on one company, strict limits.
   > Board: the recipe in a locked safe; the advantages and disadvantages as
   > two lists.
6. **Open source (4:50-6:00).** The recipe is shared: anyone may read, change
   and share it; usually free; FOSS. Linux, LibreOffice, Firefox, VLC, GIMP.
   For: free, as many copies as you like, can be changed, many people check
   it, runs on old computers. Against: community support, looks different,
   files may not match Word exactly.
   > Board: the recipe card being photocopied and handed around a crowd.
7. **Freeware and shareware (6:00-7:20).** WhatsApp is free - is it open
   source? No: freeware is free to use but the recipe stays secret. Free as
   in price vs free as in freedom (Stallman). Shareware: try before you buy
   - 30 days or features missing, then pay for a product key.
   > Board: a four-box grid - Proprietary / Open source / Freeware /
   > Shareware - Clicky drops Office, LibreOffice, Linux, WhatsApp, Chrome
   > and a 30-day video editor into the boxes.
8. **Site licence (7:20-7:50).** Thirty lab computers: one site licence -
   cheaper per computer, one agreement, legal - but only on the computers it
   covers.
   > Board: a school building with 30 small screens under one umbrella.
9. **Sign-off.** (The subscription model is in the IEB section of the
   lesson; mention in one line: "and some software you only rent - that's in
   the lesson.")

## cat10-16.2 Creative Commons and software piracy (about 6 min)

**Goes:** after the written question `w16Piracy` (end of section `#piracy`).
**The pupil can afterwards:** read a Creative Commons licence (BY, SA, NC,
ND, CC0), decide whether a use is allowed, and explain what software piracy
is and why to avoid it.
**Thumbnail:** tag `CAT · SOFTWARE`, title "Some rights *reserved*"

### Scenes

1. **Hook (0:00-0:30).** Hi, and welcome to BestLessons. Thabo finds a great
   photo of Table Mountain online. May he put it on his project poster? May
   Mr Botha put it on the bakery's adverts?
   > Board: the photo as a sticky note with "CC BY-NC" on it; two hands
   > reaching for it.
2. **Copyright (0:30-1:10).** The moment someone takes a photo, they own its
   copyright - the right to decide who may copy, share, change and sell it.
   Lesson 33 goes further.
3. **Creative Commons (1:10-3:20).** Permission given in advance: "some
   rights reserved". BY - give credit (always there); SA - share alike; NC -
   non-commercial; ND - no changes; CC0 - no rights reserved. Wikipedia is CC
   BY-SA.
   > Board: the four parts drawn as badges one at a time, each with a pink
   > highlighter band; then the spectrum from "all rights reserved" to "no
   > rights reserved".
4. **The worked example (3:20-4:00).** The CC BY-NC photo: Thabo's school
   poster - yes, with the photographer's name and the licence under it. Mr
   Botha's adverts - no, adverts make money.
   > Board: a tick on the poster, a cross on the advert.
5. **Software piracy (4:00-5:30).** Copying, installing, using, sharing or
   selling software without a valid licence: a cracked program, a copied
   product key, one single-user licence on 30 computers, a "free" paid game
   from a strange website, copies sold on flash drives. Why say no: illegal
   (fines - and the licences must be bought anyway), malware that steals
   passwords and banking details, no updates, the makers lose income. The
   legal choices: open source, freeware, trials, student prices, free web
   versions.
   > Board: the pirate-disc joke - a disc with a skull, "Bonus malware
   > included"; then a list of the legal choices with green ticks.
6. **Sign-off.**
