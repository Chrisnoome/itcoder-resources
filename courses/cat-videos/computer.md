Lesson: `content/cattheory10/computer.php`. Three short videos, one per main
idea. Board in the CAT marker style with Clicky (brand/cat-art-style.md);
yellow highlighter on any words on screen being talked about. No screen

## cat10-01.1 What makes a computer a computer (about 5 min)

**Goes:** after section `#computer` - the comment after `t1Embedded`.
**The pupil can afterwards:** give the five parts of the definition of a computer, test any device against them, and say what an embedded computer is.
**Thumbnail:** tag `CAT · BASICS`, title "Is it a *computer*?"

### Scenes

1. **Hook (0:00-0:35).** Hi, and welcome to BestLessons. Ask ten people what a computer is, and most give you a list: a laptop, a desktop, a tablet. But a list is not a definition. A definition has to work for a computer nobody has built yet.
   > Board: a laptop, a desktop and a tablet drawn in a row; Clicky in front of them, scratching his head, a "?" beside him.
2. **The five parts (0:35-1:50).** A computer is electronic, multi-purpose, accepts input, processes it, and produces output. Nothing about a screen, a keyboard, the size or the price - the definition is about what it does, not what it looks like.
   > Board: five sticky-note boxes appear one by one with the five words. Yellow highlighter on each as it is said.
3. **Multi-purpose (1:50-2:30).** The part people often leave out. A laptop is a word processor one minute and a music player the next - only the instructions changed.
   > Board: one laptop; its screen swaps from a page of text to a music player. Pink highlighter on "multi-purpose".
4. **The five-question test (2:30-3:40).** Ask the five questions one at a time. The wind-up watch: not electronic, no data in, one job - not a computer. The basic calculator: electronic, input from the keys, it calculates, it shows the answer - but it only does sums. Not multi-purpose, so it is a special-purpose device.
   > Board: two columns, a watch and a calculator; ticks and crosses down five rows. The calculator's single cross on "multi-purpose" gets the yellow highlighter.
5. **The chip inside (3:40-4:40).** Look inside the calculator: a tiny chip running one fixed program. A computer hidden inside another device, doing one job, is an embedded computer. The washing machine, step by step: you choose the cycle and press Start (input); sensors measure the water and the temperature (input); the chip follows its program - fill, heat, wash, rinse, spin (processing); it beeps (output). The machine only washes; the chip is the computer.
   > Board: a washing machine with its four steps numbered beside it, each labelled I, I, P, O.
6. **Sign-off (4:40-5:00).** Microwaves, cars, TV remotes and traffic lights all have one. You are surrounded by computers you never see.

## cat10-01.2 Input, processing, output - and two more (about 7 min)

**Goes:** after section `#storage` - the comment after `t1Cycle`.
**The pupil can afterwards:** take any task apart into input, processing and output, add storage and communication, and use both names for the model.
**Thumbnail:** tag `CAT · BASICS`, title "In, *work*, out"

### Scenes

1. **Hook (0:00-0:45).** Hi, and welcome to BestLessons. Making a cup of tea is a system. In: water, a teabag, milk and sugar. The work: boiling, letting it draw, stirring. Out: a cup of tea - and a soggy teabag.
   > Board: a kettle, a mug and a teabag; three arrows labelled In, Work, Out.
2. **The IPO model (0:45-2:00).** A computer is a system too. Input - data and instructions go in: typed, tapped, scanned, spoken, or measured by a sensor. Processing - the computer calculates, compares, sorts and searches. Output - the result comes out on a screen, on paper, through a speaker, or as a message.
   > Board: three boxes in a row, INPUT - PROCESSING - OUTPUT, each with two small examples drawn under it.
3. **One done for you (2:00-2:50).** Thabo works out 12 x 15 on his phone's calculator. He taps 1, 2, x, 1, 5 and = (input). The phone multiplies (processing). 180 appears (output). Any task makes sense if you ask: what goes in, what is done to it, what comes out?
   > Board: a phone with the keys tapped; the three steps numbered. Yellow highlighter on "180".
4. **Storage and communication (2:50-4:30).** Two more jobs make a computer useful. Storage: you save your essay, switch off, and open it the next day. Communication: sending a WhatsApp, loading a web page, backing up your photos. All five together: the information processing cycle. The arrows between processing and storage go both ways - the computer writes when you save, and reads when you open.
   > Board: the ipo-cycle drawing built up; the two-way arrow drawn last, with Clicky pointing at it: "both ways!"
5. **Two names, one idea (4:30-5:20).** The IEB calls it the IPO model. CAPS calls it the information processing cycle, and adds storage and communication. Know both.
   > Board: the drawing split by a highlighter swipe - blue over the top row (IPO), pink over all five (the cycle).
6. **On your phone (5:20-6:20).** You type a message (input). The phone checks the spelling and adds the time (processing). It appears in the chat (output). The chat is saved (storage). It is sent to your friend's phone (communication).
   > Board: the Learn / Memorise table's last column, row by row.
7. **The trap (6:20-6:50).** The input is not the input device. At an ATM, the PIN you type is the input; the keypad is the device.
   > Board: a keypad with "PIN 4 7 1 9" on a sticky note. Yellow highlighter on the PIN, a cross through "keypad = input".
8. **Sign-off.**

## cat10-01.3 Garbage in, garbage out (about 4 min)

**Goes:** after section `#gigo` - the comment after `w1Gigo`.
**The pupil can afterwards:** explain GIGO in their own words, apply it to a situation, and name ways to get data in correctly.
**Thumbnail:** tag `CAT · BASICS`, title "*37* or 73?"

### Scenes

1. **Hook (0:00-0:30).** Hi, and welcome to BestLessons. A computer does not check whether your data makes sense. It does exactly what it is told with what it is given - and then shows the result in a neat table that looks completely official.
   > Board: Clicky holding up a very official-looking report, a stamp on it.
2. **One wrong number (0:30-1:50).** Follow it through a school's marks system. A clerk types 37 instead of 73 - the input is wrong. The class average is worked out correctly, with the wrong number. The class is ranked correctly, with the wrong number. The report is printed: neat, official - and wrong. Nothing in the computer noticed.
   > Board: the gigo-37-73 drawing, step by step. Yellow highlighter on "37"; each later step gets a tick, and the last a big cross.
3. **The name (1:50-2:30).** Garbage in, garbage out - GIGO. Wrong data in means wrong information out, however good the program is.
   > Board: "GIGO" in Permanent Marker, each letter opened out into its word.
4. **A spacecraft (2:30-3:10).** In 1999 NASA lost the Mars Climate Orbiter. One team's software gave the engine's thrust in pound-force seconds; the other team's expected newton-seconds. Nobody converted, and it flew too close to Mars.
   > Board: a small spacecraft drawn heading for Mars, on a path that is too low. Note: keep the units on screen exactly as written here.
5. **Getting data in correctly (3:10-3:50).** That is why so much of CAT is about input: a drop-down list instead of typing; a check that refuses a date of birth in the future; a second person checking what was captured.
   > Board: three small drawings - a drop-down list, a date box with a red cross, two people looking at one screen.
6. **Sign-off.**
