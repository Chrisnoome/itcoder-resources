## cat10-05.1 What's inside the box (about 7 min)

**Goes:** after section `#motherboard` (the comment is above "Explore it: a real motherboard").
**The pupil can afterwards:** say what hardware is, name the parts inside the system unit, and say what the motherboard is for.
**Thumbnail:** tag `CAT · HARDWARE`, title "What's *inside* the box?"

### Scenes

1. **Hook (0:00-0:40).** Hi, and welcome to BestLessons. Lerato has the old
   family computer open on the kitchen table, and Gogo Dlamini looks inside:
   "So this is where the Internet lives." It isn't. Let's find out what does.
   > Board: a desktop box with its side panel off, a screwdriver and a teapot; Clicky peeking over the edge of the case.
2. **What hardware is (0:40-1:50).** Hardware is all the physical parts of a
   computer system - the parts you can touch. The drop-it-on-your-foot test:
   the phone is hardware, the song on it is not.
   > Board: a phone and a music note; Clicky drops the phone on his foot ("ow - hardware"), the note floats past him. Yellow highlighter on "the parts you can touch".
3. **Five jobs (1:50-2:50).** Hardware is sorted by its job: input, processing,
   output, storage and communication devices - one example of each.
   > Board: five boxes in a row, each with a quick drawing (keyboard, CPU chip, screen, flash drive, router).
4. **The system unit (2:50-4:00).** The box is the system unit, not "the
   CPU" - the CPU is one chip the size of a postage stamp. Inside: the
   motherboard, the CPU under its fan, RAM and ROM, the storage drive, the
   power supply and fans. A laptop or phone packs the same parts into one
   body.
   > Board: the case drawn open; each part appears and is labelled as it is named; a red "X" over a label "CPU" stuck on the whole box, and a pink highlighter on "system unit".
5. **The motherboard (4:00-6:10).** The big flat board that joins every part
   so they can send data to each other - a city's roads, with the CPU, the
   memory and the storage as buildings. On it: the CPU socket, the RAM slots
   right next to it, the ROM chip, the ports at the back, cables to the
   drives and the power supply.
   > Board: the motherboard drawn as a map of roads; Clicky drives a tiny car of data from the "RAM" building to the "CPU" building.
   > Screen: the lesson's motherboard photo (`ex-g10-motherboard.jpg`), zoomed slowly from the CPU socket to the RAM slots to the back ports, each ringed in yellow highlighter.
6. **Sign-off (6:10-6:40).** Recap in three lines: hardware is what you can
   touch; the system unit is the box; the motherboard joins it all. Next: the
   CPU, RAM and ROM.

## cat10-05.2 CPU, RAM and ROM (about 8 min)

**Goes:** after section `#memVsStorage` (the comment is below the `s5Storage` question).
**The pupil can afterwards:** say what the CPU, RAM and ROM each do, read GHz and cores in an advert, and explain the difference between memory and storage.
**Thumbnail:** tag `CAT · HARDWARE`, title "The brain, the counter and the *cupboard*"

### Scenes

1. **Hook (0:00-0:30).** Hi, and welcome to BestLessons. Mr Botha's old
   laptop freezes when he has three programs open. An advert says "8 cores,
   4.4 GHz, 16 GB RAM, 512 GB SSD". By the end you will read every word of it.
   > Board: the advert on a yellow sticky note; Clicky squinting at it with a "?".
2. **The CPU (0:30-1:40).** The chip that does the work: it follows the
   program's instructions, calculating, comparing and deciding. Excel's sum
   and the game's "did you hit the wall?" are the CPU. It hides under a heat
   sink and fan because it gets very hot.
   > Board: a chip under metal fins and a fan, little heat squiggles rising.
3. **GHz (1:40-2:50).** The CPU works to the beat of a clock. Giga means
   billion; hertz means times per second. 2.5 GHz is 2 500 000 000 ticks a
   second - count one a second and it takes you about 80 years.
   > Board: a ticking clock; the number 2 500 000 000 written out digit by digit with yellow highlighter; Clicky with a grey beard, still counting.
4. **Cores (2:50-3:40).** A core is one complete processor; a 4-core chip has
   four. Two cores: two brains in one head. Each works on something different
   at the same time - music, browser, virus check. Phones have about 8;
   laptops 4 to 16. GHz is how fast each core is; cores is how many work at once.
   > Board: a head with two brains; then four small brains each holding a different task.
5. **RAM (3:40-5:00).** The working memory: it holds the programs and work open
   right now. The kitchen counter (RAM) and the cupboard (storage). Opening an
   essay: double-click, Word is copied into RAM, the essay is copied into RAM,
   the CPU works on them there. Volatile: a power cut wipes it - the unsaved
   paragraph is gone. More RAM, more open at once without slowing down; 8 GB
   for schoolwork, 16 GB comfortable.
   > Board: the kitchen - counter and cupboard; Word and essay.docx carried from cupboard to counter; then the lights go out (load shedding) and the counter is wiped clean.
6. **ROM (5:00-6:00).** RAM is empty when you switch on, so the first
   instructions come from ROM: a small chip that keeps its contents without
   power (non-volatile), read but almost never changed. The microwave keeps
   its program after a power cut, even with the clock flashing 12:00.
   > Board: the RAM/ROM table built row by row; pink highlighter on "non-volatile".
7. **Switching on - IEB (6:00-6:40).** For IEB pupils (and anyone curious): the
   start-up program in ROM is the BIOS. It checks the hardware (the POST),
   finds the operating system, loads it into RAM and hands over. That is booting.
   > Board: five numbered steps from a power button to a log-in screen; a small "IEB" tag in the corner.
8. **Memory vs storage (6:40-7:40).** Memory (RAM, primary) holds what you work
   on now: fast, expensive, volatile. Storage (secondary) keeps files: slower,
   cheaper, non-volatile. Open copies into RAM; Save copies back. A memory card
   is storage, whatever its name says. Back to the advert: 16 GB is memory;
   512 GB is storage.
   > Board: the advert from scene 1, each line highlighted as it is explained.
9. **Sign-off.**
