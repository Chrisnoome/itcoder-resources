# CAT 11 lesson 10: LANs and WLANs - videos

Lesson: `content/cattheory11/lanwlan.php`. Two videos: the LAN and the WLAN
compared, and how a LAN's parts work together (with client-server and
peer-to-peer). Board in the CAT marker style with Clicky
(brand/cat-art-style.md); yellow highlighter on any words on screen being
talked about. Everything below is in the lesson text. No screen recordings
needed.

## cat11-10.1 LAN or WLAN? (about 7 min)

**Goes:** after section `#compare` - the comment after `g10CableOrWifi`.
**The pupil can afterwards:** give the definition, purpose, role and uses of a LAN and a WLAN, an advantage, a disadvantage and a limitation of each, and choose cable or Wi-Fi for a device.
**Thumbnail:** tag `CAT · NETWORKS`, title "Cable or *Wi-Fi*?"

### Scenes

1. **Hook (0:00-0:40).** Hi, and welcome to BestLessons. Phumlani Secondary has been given a new lab and Wi-Fi for every classroom. The installer's list: 30 desktops and a server, two switches, 32 cables, a fibre cable, six access points. Thabo asks: why cables for the lab, but Wi-Fi for the classrooms?
   > Board: the installer's list on a clipboard; Clicky pointing at "6 access points".
2. **The LAN in four words (0:40-2:00).** Definition: a network on one site of one organisation. Purpose: to communicate and share files, printers, programs and one Internet connection. Role: one system, managed from one place. Uses: a school, a hospital, a bank branch, a supermarket's tills.
   > Board: four boxes labelled Definition, Purpose, Role, Uses, filled one at a time. Yellow highlighter on each word.
3. **LAN: good, bad, built-in (2:00-3:20).** Advantages: work on the server opens anywhere; shared printers and Internet; one backup; log-ins and updates from one place; fast, steady cables. Disadvantages: cost; an administrator; the server fails and everyone stops; viruses spread. Limitations (built in, can't be fixed by using it better): one site; a cable about 100 m; a switch's ports run out.
   > Board: three columns; a pink highlighter on the word "built in".
4. **The WLAN (3:20-5:00).** A LAN, or part of one, joined by radio waves (Wi-Fi) through access points cabled to a switch - the wireless part of the same LAN. For devices that move. Classrooms, a hospital ward, Mr Botha's bakery. Advantages: move about; no cables; easy to add; phones and tablets can join. Disadvantages: less secure - the signal reaches the car park; interference - even a microwave; slower and less steady. Limitations: tens of metres; one access point shared by the whole class; every device needs a wireless NIC.
   > Board: the school with Wi-Fi rings from ceiling access points, the rings spilling into the car park.
5. **Side by side (5:00-6:20).** The Learn table: joins by cable to a switch / by radio to an access point; wired NIC / wireless NIC; stays put / moves. Most organisations use both. Thabo's answer: the lab desktops never move and need a steady connection each; laptops move.
   > Board: the table drawn row by row; then the four devices from the sort question, each dragged to Cable or Wi-Fi.
6. **Sign-off.**

## cat11-10.2 What a LAN is made of (about 8 min)

**Goes:** after section `#clientServer` - the comment after `r10WhyServer`.
**The pupil can afterwards:** name the parts of a LAN and the role of each, follow a file, a print job and a web page through them, tell the switch from the router, and compare client-server with peer-to-peer.
**Thumbnail:** tag `CAT · NETWORKS`, title "One network, *eight* parts"

### Scenes

1. **Hook (0:00-0:30).** Hi, and welcome to BestLessons. In Grade 10 you met the boxes one at a time. Today they work together.
   > Board: the parts scattered on the board, Clicky trying to plug them together.
2. **The parts and their roles (0:30-2:30).** Workstations; the server; the NIC - wired (a port) or wireless (a radio), a laptop has both, a desktop can add a USB dongle; the switch at the centre of the wired LAN; the wireless access point cabled to a switch; the router to the Internet; the modem or fibre box; the media - UTP (copper, about 100 m), fibre (light, kilometres), radio waves.
   > Board: the nh-school-network drawing built up part by part; yellow highlighter on each name as it is said.
3. **Thabo saves, prints and browses (2:30-4:40).** Save: NIC, cable, switch, only the server's port, his folder - open it anywhere tomorrow. Print: to the switch, to the server's queue, back through the switch to the printer. A website: switch, router (the site is not on the school's network), fibre box, Internet - and back the same way. Lerato's laptop: the first step is radio to an access point.
   > Board: a dot travelling the path three times, each step numbered.
4. **Switch or router? (4:40-5:20).** The switch joins the devices inside the LAN and sends data only where it is meant to go. The router joins the LAN to another network - the Internet.
   > Board: two circles, "inside" and "outside"; the switch in the middle of one, the router on the line between them.
5. **Client-server or peer-to-peer (5:20-7:30).** Phumlani's lab has a server; Mr Botha's two computers and a printer do not. Client-server: servers provide files, log-ins and printing; clients ask. Central files and backup, one administrator; costs more, server failure stops everyone. Peer-to-peer: no server, every computer equal, each shares its own. Cheap and simple; files spread out, no central control, a sharing computer slows down or switches off. Peer-to-peer is how it is run, not how it is connected.
   > Board: the client-server-p2p drawing; then the bakery's two computers sharing one printer. Yellow highlighter on "how it is run".
6. **Sign-off.**
