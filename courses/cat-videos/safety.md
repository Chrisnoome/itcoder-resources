## cat10-30.1 Logging in and strong passwords (about 7 min)

**Goes:** after section `#passwords` - the comment after `w30Passphrase`.
**The pupil can afterwards:** explain authentication (username and verification), name the ways to verify, and say what makes a password strong.
**Thumbnail:** tag `CAT · SAFETY`, title "Is your password *strong*?"

### Scenes

1. **Hook (0:00-0:40).** Hi, and welcome to BestLessons. Every time you unlock your phone or log in at school, you prove who you are. That is authentication.
   > Board: a phone lock screen and a school login box side by side; Clicky knocking on the login box.
2. **Two parts (0:40-1:50).** The username says who you claim to be - often your e-mail or school number, not a secret. The verification proves it: a password, a PIN, a pattern, face recognition or a fingerprint - biometrics.
   > Board: two sticky notes, USERNAME and VERIFICATION; five small drawings under verification.
   > Screen: (optional) the VM's Settings > Accounts > Sign-in options page, held still, yellow highlighter on PIN, Password and Facial recognition.
3. **Thabo logs in (1:50-2:40).** He types thabo.10c; he types his password; the server checks the two belong together; it lets him in, and shows only his own files. On his phone, the fingerprint does it.
   > Board: the four numbered steps.
4. **Why passwords fall (2:40-3:20).** Criminals' computers try billions of guesses a second, and lists of leaked passwords first.
   > Board: a counter spinning past billions.
5. **Strong (3:20-4:40).** Long - 15 characters or more. Unique to one account. Not personal. Not predictable - not 123456 or a word with 1! on the end. Secret - not on a sticky note on the screen.
   > Board: five ticks; the password-note doodle redrawn, with a cross.
6. **Which is stronger? (4:40-6:00).** Sipho2009! looks strong but is 10 characters: a name, a year and "!". braai-giraffe-umbrella-seven is 28 characters of random words - far too many combinations to try, and easier to remember. That is a passphrase. A password manager keeps a different one for every account.
   > Board: the two passwords on sticky notes; the long one gets the pink highlighter.
7. **Sign-off.**

## cat10-30.2 OTPs and two kinds of proof (about 5 min)

**Goes:** after section `#multilevel` - the comment after `q30Otp`.
**The pupil can afterwards:** name the three kinds of proof, explain multi-level authentication, and say what an OTP is and why it is never shared.
**Thumbnail:** tag `CAT · SAFETY`, title "*Never* read it out"

### Scenes

1. **Hook (0:00-0:50).** Hi, and welcome to BestLessons. Tuesday, 18:20. Gogo Dlamini's phone gets an SMS: "Your one-time PIN is 481223. Never share this PIN." Then a call from "the bank" asking her to read it out. "The SMS says never share it," she says - and puts the phone down.
   > Board: Gogo's phone with the SMS; a speech bubble from the caller; Clicky cheering.
2. **Three kinds of proof (0:50-2:00).** Something you know - a password, PIN or pattern. Something you have - your phone, your bank card. Something you are - your fingerprint or face.
   > Board: the protecting-mfa drawing, built a column at a time.
3. **Multi-level authentication (2:00-2:50).** Ask for two or more different kinds, and a stolen password is not enough. Also called two-factor, two-step or multi-factor. Two of the same kind don't count.
   > Board: a password plus a phone = a tick; a password plus a PIN = a cross ("both KNOW").
4. **The OTP (2:50-4:10).** A one-time PIN works once, for a few minutes, and proves you have your phone. Lerato buys a bus ticket: card details; the bank sends an OTP; she types it in; the bank checks it; five minutes later it's useless.
   > Board: the five steps, a clock beside the code ticking to zero.
5. **The rule (4:10-4:40).** Never give an OTP to anyone - the bank already knows it. Hang up and phone the number on the back of your card.
   > Board: the warning in big marker letters; yellow highlighter on "never".
6. **Sign-off.**

## cat10-30.3 Your personal information and the POPI Act (about 6 min)

**Goes:** after the CAPS section `#popi` - the comment after the board section ends.
**The pupil can afterwards:** list personal information, say why criminals want it and how to protect it, and (CAPS) say what the POPI Act is for, what an organisation must do and who to complain to.
**Thumbnail:** tag `CAT · SAFETY`, title "Think *before* you post"

### Scenes

1. **Hook (0:00-0:40).** Hi, and welcome to BestLessons. Thabo is about to post: his address, his birthday, his new number, a photo of his ID card - and "away in Durban 20 to 28 December".
   > Board: Thabo's post drawn as a phone screen; Clicky pulling at his sleeve.
2. **Personal information (0:40-1:40).** Anything that could identify you: name, ID number, date of birth, address, phone number, e-mail, where you are, photos, fingerprint, marks, bank and medical details.
   > Board: the list as sticky notes around a silhouette.
3. **Why criminals want it (1:40-2:30).** Name, ID number and address: identity theft. Birthday and the dog's name: guessed passwords. "Away all December": an empty house.
   > Board: three arrows from the post to a thief, each labelled.
4. **Protect it (2:30-3:30).** Think before you post; private accounts; no ID number, address, phone number or travel plans, and never a photo of your ID or bank card; location sharing off; give out only what is needed.
   > Board: Thabo's post with the five items circled in turn (as in mw30Overshare).
5. **The POPI Act (3:30-5:30).** (CAPS.) Who makes sure organisations look after your information? The Protection of Personal Information Act - POPIA - law since 2013, followed since July 2021. Organisations must collect only what they need and say why; use it only for that; keep it accurate and safe; not keep it too long; report leaks; send adverts only to people who agreed (or customers), and stop when asked. Children's information is protected more strictly. You may see and correct what is held about you, and complain to the Information Regulator, which can fine up to R10 million.
   > Board: a school, a clinic and a shop, each holding a folder with a padlock; a sticky note "POPI Act"; the Information Regulator as a judge's gavel.
6. **Sign-off.**
